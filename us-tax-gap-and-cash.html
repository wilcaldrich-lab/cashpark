The U.S. tax gap and cash-visible income

What the IRS actually measures, and why income that never shows up on a W-2 or 1099 is where most individual underreporting sits. A compliance fact, not a tip sheet.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. Figures are IRS projections for tax year 2022 from Publication 5869, unless noted. They assume compliance behavior has not changed since the IRS’s tax years 2014–2016 estimates.

The fall 2025 update is still delayed. The IRS’s own tax-gap page, checked on Nov 25, 2026, still lists “Fall of calendar year 2025—new tax gap estimates and projections (delayed).” No newer official release has replaced Publication 5869. The numbers on this page are the October 2024 projections for tax year 2022, not a 2025 or 2026 estimate.

What the IRS actually measures

The gross tax gap is the amount of true tax liability that is not paid voluntarily and on time. The net tax gap is the part of that amount the IRS does not expect to recover through enforcement or other late payments. These are official IRS projections, not a cashscoreboard.com calculation.

The $696 billion gross tax gap has three components:

  • Underreporting — $539 billion. Tax understated on timely filed returns. This is by far the largest piece.
  • Underpayment — $94 billion. Tax reported on time but not paid on time.
  • Nonfiling — $63 billion. Tax not paid on time by people who do not file on time.

By type of tax, the same $696 billion is: individual income tax $514 billion, employment tax $127 billion, corporate tax $50 billion, and estate tax $5 billion. After late and enforced payments, the net tax gap is $447 billion for individual income tax, $119 billion for employment taxes, $40 billion for corporate tax, and less than $0.5 billion each for estate and excise tax.

Source: IRS, Publication 5869, Tax Gap Projections for Tax Year 2022 (October 2024); also summarized on IRS, The tax gap.

Where income is hard to see, misreporting is much higher

The IRS’s long-standing finding is that compliance is higher when income is reported to the IRS by a third party, and higher still when it is also subject to withholding. The agency calls this “visibility.”

The net misreporting percentages below are from the tax years 2014–2016 estimates, which still underlie the 2022 projections:

  • About 1 percent of income is misreported when there is substantial information reporting and withholding (wages are the example).
  • About 6 percent when there is substantial information reporting but no withholding.
  • About 55 percent for income with little or no information reporting, such as nonfarm sole-proprietor income.

For the 2022 projections, income items subject to little or no information reporting account for $179 billion, which is 26 percent of the gross tax gap and 47 percent of individual income-tax underreporting. Nonfarm proprietor income alone is $117 billion of that $179 billion (17 percent of the gross tax gap, 31 percent of individual underreporting). The other pieces in that low-visibility group are rents and royalties ($28 billion), “other income” ($21 billion), farm income ($7 billion), and Form 4797 income ($5 billion).

Wages, salaries, and tips — the high-visibility group with withholding — account for $9 billion of the projected individual underreporting tax gap, or 1 percent of the gross tax gap.

Source: IRS, Publication 5869, Tax Gap Projections for Tax Year 2022 (October 2024), Table 4 and the visibility discussion in the executive summary and Figure 3.

There is no official “cash tax gap” line. The IRS does not publish a separate estimate of tax lost specifically on cash transactions. A lot of underreporting sits in categories where cash is common, especially nonfarm proprietor income. That is a fact about visibility, not a license to treat every cash business as noncompliant, and it is not a how-to. This page does not advise anyone on how to keep income off a form.

How to read a projection

Publication 5869 is explicit: these projections “are predictions of the tax gap accounting for changes in the numbers and types of returns assuming compliance behavior remains the same from many years prior. They are not predictions of compliance behavior.” The IRS uses them as a bridge until newer audit data can support a full estimate. Until the delayed 2025 release appears, tax year 2022 is the latest official picture.
