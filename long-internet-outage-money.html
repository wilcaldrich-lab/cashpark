What happens to your money in a long outage

Card and phone payments feel instant until the processor, the power, or the network is down. Here is what has actually happened, what the laws do and do not fix, and how much cash is worth keeping.

Last checked Nov 24, 2026. Where a point is our reasoning rather than a sourced fact, we say so.

A real example: June 23, 2026, in the UK

On June 23, 2026, a power-grid disruption hit payment processor Worldpay. Worldpay said it caused "intermittent transaction authorization issues" for some clients. Contactless payments failed at a number of stores, including some Tesco branches, during an England World Cup game, when pubs were busy. Some pubs and venues said they were taking cash only, and videos showed long queues at cash machines. Tesco said the issue was later resolved. (The Guardian, June 23, 2026)

That was a UK event, and it was intermittent rather than a total blackout. We use it as a reminder, not a forecast. It also shows both sides: the places that could still take cash kept selling, and the cash machines were what people rushed to.

What a cash-acceptance law does and does not fix

A law that says a store must take cash helps if the store's card terminal is down but the register and the lights work. Several laws spell out the machine case. New York City, D.C., Connecticut, and Maryland each let a store skip cash if it provides a cash-to-prepaid-card machine, and require the store to take cash if that machine breaks (NYC Admin. Code, D.C. Code ch. 54, C.G.S. § 21a-434, Maryland ch. 714).

But laws have gaps in exactly this situation:

  • Illinois's law, which starts Jan 1, 2028, exempts stores that cannot take cash because of a sales-system failure or a temporary insufficiency of cash on hand, and it does not apply to self-service checkouts or to overnight hours, 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. (HB 4592).
  • D.C.'s law does not apply during a public health emergency declared by the Mayor, and temporary language adds notice-based exceptions for some alcohol-licensed retailers and late-night restaurants (D.C. Code § 28-5404).
  • Maryland's law covers essential goods only and applies between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m.
  • New Jersey exempts some parking facilities and, for sports or entertainment venues of 10,000+ seats, the law does not apply (N.J.S.A. 56:8-2.33).

And no cash-acceptance law makes a power-dead store open its register, makes an ATM work, or makes a bank branch available. Those are outside the laws' scope.

What stays out of reach in an outage (our reasoning)

This part is common sense, not sourced data, so treat it that way. In a long outage of power or networks, the things that tend to fail together are card terminals, ATMs, mobile banking, and any app-based payment. Paper cash and a place that is still open and can make change keep working. That is the whole case for a small reserve. It is not a case for hoarding.

How much cash is worth keeping

  • The Fed found a lot of people already do this. 76% of consumers carried cash, averaging $69, and 45% kept an average of $364 somewhere else for savings or emergencies (Federal Reserve Financial Services, May 11, 2026). That is a useful benchmark, not a recommendation.
  • Think in days, not dollars. A common-sense starting point is a few days of essentials, such as food, water, fuel, and basic health items. That is our suggestion, not advice, and it depends on your household.
  • Small bills. Many laws let stores refuse bills above $20 (NY, Maryland, Illinois, Berkeley, Miami-Dade), and a store in a power cut may not be able to make change for a $100.

A short checklist

  • Know which of your accounts you can reach without a phone or internet (a debit card and PIN, a branch, a check).
  • Keep a small, mixed-bill cash reserve somewhere safe at home.
  • Do not put your whole emergency fund in cash. Keep it somewhere that earns and that you can reach through more than one route.
  • If a store refuses cash, look up your state or city on the tracker before arguing. The rule may not cover that kind of business.
