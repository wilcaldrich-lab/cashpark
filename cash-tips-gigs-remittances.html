Tips, gigs, and remittances

How cash still moves through everyday work and across borders: official figures on hard-to-see income, recorded remittances, and who in the United States sends money home.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. This page describes official data on how money moves. It is not advice on how to report, or not report, income.

Income that never shows up on a W-2 is where most individual underreporting sits

The IRS does not publish a figure for “cash tips that go unreported.” What it does publish is the effect of visibility. Income with little or no information reporting — a group that includes nonfarm sole-proprietor income — has a net misreporting percentage of about 55 percent in the tax years 2014–2016 estimates, compared with about 1 percent for wages that are subject to substantial information reporting and withholding. In the tax year 2022 projections, that low-visibility group accounts for $179 billion of the gross tax gap, and nonfarm proprietor income alone is $117 billion.

Those figures, and the delayed 2025 tax-gap update, are discussed with sources on The U.S. tax gap and cash-visible income. The IRS maintains separate pages on tip recordkeeping and reporting and on information returns such as Form 1099-K and Form 1099-NEC. CashPark is pointing to those pages, not rewriting the rules, and is not offering a guide to keeping tips or gig income off a form.

There is no official “percent of gig work paid in cash.” CashPark did not find a current official BLS or IRS series that states what share of gig, contract, or tipped work is paid in cash. This page does not invent one.

Remittances: the official recorded totals

The latest official World Bank / KNOMAD brief CashPark could verify is World Bank / KNOMAD, Migration and Development Brief 40, Remittances Slowed in 2023, Expected to Grow Faster in 2024 (June 2024). Table 1.1 of that brief reports officially recorded remittance flows as follows:

e = estimate, f = forecast, as labeled in the brief. In 2023, remittances to low- and middle-income countries reached an estimated $656 billion, a 0.7 percent increase, and outperformed foreign direct investment and official development assistance. The United States is identified in the brief as the largest source country for remittances. The World Bank, Remittances & Migration (page dated September 28, 2026), dated September 28, 2026, still cites the $656 billion 2023 figure for remittances back to home countries.

Sending money remains expensive. In the fourth quarter of 2023, the global average cost of sending $200 was 6.4 percent, up slightly from 6.2 percent a year earlier, according to the same brief (drawing on the World Bank’s Remittance Prices Worldwide database). Banks were the costliest channel, at an average of 12 percent; post offices averaged 7.7 percent, and money transfer operators 5.5 percent.

Official remittance totals miss informal channels. The $656 billion and $857 billion figures are officially recorded flows. They do not include money that travels outside formal reporting — for example, cash carried by hand or transferred through unregulated channels. The Bank itself has long noted that gap. CashPark is not estimating the unrecorded amount.

Brief 41 totals were not used. Secondary summaries have cited a later “Brief 41” with global remittances around $905 billion and remittances to low- and middle-income countries around $685 billion in 2024. CashPark could not verify those headline totals from an official Brief 41 PDF. One World Bank document URL that search results labeled as Brief 41 was, when downloaded, Migration and Development Brief 38 (June 2023). Until an official later brief can be checked, this page uses Brief 40.

Who in the United States sends or receives money this way

The FDIC’s 2023 household survey asked about nonbank international remittances. In 2023, 2.8 percent of all U.S. households sent or received international remittances through a nonbank provider. The share was 2.7 percent among banked households and 5.2 percent among unbanked households. It was much higher among foreign-born noncitizens (16.1 percent) and foreign-born citizens (10.3 percent) than among U.S.-born households (1.0 percent), and higher among Asian (9.2 percent) and Hispanic (7.5 percent) households than among White households (0.9 percent).

Source: FDIC, 2023 National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households (November 2024), Table 4.2. The 2025 FDIC survey report is not yet released; see Unbanked America.
