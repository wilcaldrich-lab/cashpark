Cash at home vs. a safe deposit box

What is and is not insured, what the FDIC advises, and what a stash costs you in interest at today’s verified rates.

Short answer. Cash in a home safe or a bank safe deposit box is not covered by FDIC deposit insurance, and it earns no interest. The FDIC says you are better off keeping cash in a bank deposit account than in either one. A safe deposit box is storage space from the bank, not a deposit account. If you keep a stash anyway, ask your insurance agent about coverage. Source: FDIC, Five Things to Know About Safe Deposit Boxes, Home Safes and Your Valuables (page last updated Aug 1, 2023).

More from the FDIC

  • Read the rental agreement. The bank may limit what you can keep in a safe deposit box, and the limits could include cash.
  • Protect against water. Use water-safe, zippered plastic bags or other resealable containers.
  • Do not label the key. Keep the box number and the bank’s name off or away from the key in case it is lost or stolen.
  • Joint access has limits. The bank would likely not be responsible for anything an authorized person takes without your permission.
  • Death and access. Who can open the box after the owner dies depends on state law.
  • Good box candidates are originals of key documents such as birth certificates, property deeds, and car titles, plus family keepsakes and valuable collections. For where to keep an original will, the FDIC says to ask an attorney.

Moving a lot of cash: reporting rules exist

Holding cash is not the issue. Moving or receiving large amounts can trigger federal reports, and it is illegal to split transactions to avoid them. In short, according to the agencies:

  • A business that receives more than $10,000 in cash in one transaction or related transactions must file IRS Form 8300 within 15 days (IRS, Form 8300 and reporting cash payments of over $10,000).
  • U.S. financial institutions file a Currency Transaction Report for each currency transaction over $10,000 (FDIC, Bank Secrecy Act section of the Risk Management Manual (currency transaction reports)).
  • Bringing, sending, or receiving more than $10,000 in currency or monetary instruments into or out of the United States must be reported to Customs and Border Protection (U.S. Customs and Border Protection, Money and Other Monetary Instruments).

This is a pointer, not legal or tax advice. Read the agency pages for the exact rules and exceptions.

What cashscoreboard.com is and is not saying

A small, deliberate cash reserve can make sense, and the Fed’s Diary shows many people keep one. The point of this page is that the reserve has a cost (the interest it does not earn) and a risk (it is not FDIC-insured). The scoreboard shows where the rest of your cash can earn something.
