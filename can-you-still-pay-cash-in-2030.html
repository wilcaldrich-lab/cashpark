Can you still pay cash in 2030?

Probably yes, in most places. But "yes" now depends on where you live, what you buy, and how big the bill is. Here is what the data and the laws say today, and what to do about it.

Last checked Nov 24, 2026. Every claim below links to its source.

Cash is shrinking slowly, not vanishing

The Federal Reserve's 2026 Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, based on a survey taken in October 2025, found consumers still paid with cash for about 1 in 7 payments. Cash was the third-most-used payment type for the sixth straight year, behind credit and debit cards, which made up two-thirds of payments. Four in five people had used cash in the previous 30 days, and 90% said they planned to keep using it. Use was higher among adults 55 and older, households earning under $25,000 a year, and people in rural areas, who made about nine cash payments a month versus six for suburban and urban consumers. The Fed also reported that 76% of consumers carried cash, averaging $69, and 45% kept an average of $364 elsewhere for savings or emergencies. (Federal Reserve Financial Services, May 11, 2026)

That same survey says habits have been stable for three years. So the honest answer to "will cash exist in 2030" is that nothing in these numbers points to a cliff. The risk is more local: a particular store, stadium, or city deciding not to take it.

The legal backstop is growing, and it is uneven

Pew Research Center counted at least 10 states and 10 municipalities that require in-person businesses to accept cash, with exceptions, as of Nov 17, 2026. Massachusetts was first, in 1978; it then took more than 40 years for another state to follow. New Jersey and Rhode Island passed laws in 2019, and Colorado, Connecticut, Oregon, Delaware, and Montana followed. New York's statewide law and Maryland's took effect in 2026, and Illinois enacted one that starts in January 2028. (Pew; see our law tracker for each state's text.)

Look at the details, though. Maryland's law covers essential goods only, between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m., on in-person sales from $5 to under $300 (Chapter 714). Oregon lets businesses refuse $50 and $100 bills (ORS 659A.410). New York's statewide law lets stores refuse bills above $20 (NY Attorney General). Several places let a store skip the register if it has a no-fee machine that turns cash into a prepaid card. And places can delay: King County, Wash., pushed its start date to July 1, 2027 (Ordinance 20074).

At the federal level, the Payment Choice Act has been introduced in each of the last four Congresses without becoming law, according to Pew.

Small things are already changing

The U.S. Mint stopped producing the penny on Nov 12, 2025. New York City's consumer agency says businesses may round cash totals to the nearest nickel, but may not refuse pennies, charge more for cash, or hide a rounding policy, because the city's cash-acceptance law still applies (NYC DCWP). Maryland's law also lets merchants collect up to 4 cents extra when rounding an essential good's price in line with applicable law.

Why 2030 shows up in the digital-dollar debate

A different 2030 date is worth knowing. A federal law enacted July 11, 2026 bars the Federal Reserve from issuing a central bank digital currency, or anything substantially similar, until Dec 31, 2030 (12 U.S.C. § 423). The ban is narrow: it defines a CBDC as a dollar-denominated digital asset that is a direct Federal Reserve liability and widely available to the public, with an exception for an open, permissionless, private dollar currency that preserves the privacy of physical cash. Some states have passed their own limits too (see the tracker). None of this changes whether a store has to take paper money today. It is a separate fight about what a "digital dollar" would be.

What it means for your cash pile

  • Keep most idle cash where it earns something. Cash in a drawer earns nothing. Loading the current top savings rate… See the scoreboard.
  • Keep a small physical reserve if you want one. The Fed found nearly half of consumers keep a stash outside their wallet, averaging $364. Whether you need that is personal, and it is not a substitute for an emergency fund you can reach digitally.
  • Carry small bills. Several laws let a store refuse bills above $20, so $20s and below are the ones that work everywhere.
  • Know your local rule. If a place refuses cash, check the tracker and the linked official complaint page for that jurisdiction.

What we do not know

Nobody can say what share of payments will be cash in 2030, and we will not guess. If the Fed's next Diary shows a break from the three-year pattern, or a big state passes or repeals a law, we will update this page and the tracker.
