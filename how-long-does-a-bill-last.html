Official lifespans by denomination, what to do with a worn, torn, or burned note, and the condition words collectors use.

How long does a bill last?

When currency is deposited with a Federal Reserve Bank, processing equipment checks every note. Notes in good condition go back into circulation. Notes that fail the quality criteria are destroyed. That process determines a note’s lifespan, which varies by denomination and by how people use it. Larger notes such as the $100 are often used as a store of value, so they pass between users less often than lower denominations like the $5, which are more often used for transactions. The Fed updated its method in 2025. Sources: U.S. Currency Education Program, Lifespan Data; Federal Reserve, How long is the lifespan of U.S. paper money?.

What happens to worn-out bills

The Federal Reserve inspects deposited notes for authenticity and fitness at about 100,000 notes an hour. Notes no longer fit for circulation, usually soiled, defaced, or torn, are destroyed. On average about 15% of the currency notes deposited are destroyed because they do not meet fitness guidelines. Source: Federal Reserve Financial Services, Currency.

Paper currency is built to last. It is 75% cotton and 25% linen, and it takes about 4,000 double folds to tear a note (U.S. Currency Education Program, Currency Facts).

Worn, torn, or mutilated: who handles it

  • Unfit. BEP calls currency unfit for further circulation when it is dirty, defaced, limp, torn, or worn. Unfit currency should not be sent to BEP and may be exchanged at a commercial financial institution. (BEP, Mutilated Currency FAQs)
  • Mutilated. Currency damaged so that one-half or less of the original note remains, or whose condition makes its value questionable. Common causes are fire, water, chemicals, explosives, animal, insect, or rodent damage, and petrification or deterioration by burying. (BEP, Mutilated Currency Redemption)
  • Redemption at full value. BEP can redeem at full value when clearly more than 50% of an identifiable note is present with enough of its security features, or when 50% or less is present and the method of mutilation and the supporting evidence show to BEP’s satisfaction that the missing portions were totally destroyed. BEP’s Director has the final say. (BEP, Mutilated Currency Redemption)
  • It is free but slow. Redemption is a free public service. Standard requests can take 6 to 36 months. BEP gets more than 22,000 requests a year, with an estimated value above $35 million. (BEP, Mutilated Currency Redemption; BEP, Mutilated Currency FAQs)
  • Do not mail it to the Fed. The Federal Reserve says consumers should not send any currency directly to the Fed, including mutilated currency. (Federal Reserve, What should I do if I have damaged or mutilated currency?)
  • No redemption where a submission shows a pattern of intentional mutilation, an attempt to defraud the government, a material misrepresentation, or looks tied to a criminal scheme. (BEP, Mutilated Currency Redemption)

Condition words collectors use

Collectors describe a note’s condition with adjectives, and grading companies attach numbers to them on a 70-point scale. These are private industry standards, not government grades. CashPark does not grade notes or put prices on them. PMG’s published chart:

PMG notes that grades under Good 4 are generally not collectible unless the note is rare. PCGS Banknote also uses a 70-point numerical scale, and says that notes graded 65 and higher must also meet its Premium Paper Quality (PPQ) criteria. Each company applies its own standards for centering, margins, and handling. Sources: PMG, What is an Adjectival Grade?; PCGS Banknote, Grading Standards.
