How to read a U.S. bill

What the letters and numbers on a Federal Reserve note mean, with a decoder you can try. Sourced to the Bureau of Engraving and Printing.

Serial number decoder

Type the serial number from the front of the note. Nothing is sent anywhere; the decoding runs in your browser. The example serials below are made up.

Use a star (*) for star notes. Spaces and hyphens are ignored.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. Source for everything below unless noted: Bureau of Engraving and Printing, Serial Numbers.

Where the serial number and the other markings are

Every Federal Reserve note carries its own serial number on the front. Near the signatures is the series year, and the Federal Reserve Bank seal shows a letter and a number for the issuing bank. The series year is not the year the bill was printed or received.

Two serial formats

  • One letter, eight digits, one letter (for example B12345678A). Federal Reserve notes used this form through Series 1995. BEP says that now only the $1 and $2 notes still use it. The first letter is the issuing Federal Reserve Bank, A through L.
  • Two letters, eight digits, one letter (for example MB12345678A). Beginning with Series 1996, the first letter marks the series and the second letter is the issuing bank.

The last letter moves through the alphabet when all eight-digit numbers have been printed for a bank in a series, and starts over at A when the series changes. BEP does not use the letter O, because it looks like a zero, and does not use Z, because it is reserved for test printings.

Star notes

When an imperfect sheet is found after the serial numbers have been overprinted, it is replaced with a “star” sheet. Reusing the exact serial number would be costly and slow, so a star note has its own special serial number with a star where the last letter would be. The serial of the imperfect note is not used again in that sequence. The U.S. Currency Education Program describes star notes as replacements for defective notes. Sources: Bureau of Engraving and Printing, Serial Numbers; BEP, Currency Notes (PDF); U.S. Currency Education Program FAQ.

A star by itself does not make a note valuable. Collectors look at how many notes were in that star run. BEP publishes monthly production reports that list star and regular serial ranges by denomination, series, and bank: BEP Monthly Production Reports. cashscoreboard.com's decoder does not look up print runs yet.

Fancy serial patterns: collector convention, not official

Hobby dealers and collectors give names to eye-catching eight-digit numbers. These are not official BEP or Federal Reserve categories, there is no official price for any of them, and definitions vary. The decoder checks six common ones:

  • Solid: all eight digits the same.
  • Radar: reads the same forward and backward.
  • Repeater: the first four digits repeat as the last four.
  • Ladder: eight digits climbing or falling by one each step.
  • Binary: only the digits 0 and 1.
  • Low serial: a number of 100 or less. Collectors’ cutoffs run from the first few notes up to about 1,000.

What the decoder does not do

  • It does not give a value, an estimate, or a “rarity” grade. BEP says it does not appraise old or rare currency and suggests a currency dealer or numismatic organization (Bureau of Engraving and Printing, FAQs).
  • It does not know print-run sizes, errors, large-size notes, or small-size notes before Series 1996.
  • It cannot tell you whether a note is genuine. See how to spot a counterfeit.
