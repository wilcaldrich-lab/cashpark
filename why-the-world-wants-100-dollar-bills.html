Why the world still wants $100 bills

Most of the value of U.S. cash is in $100 notes, and a large share of those notes is estimated to sit outside the United States — as a store of value, not as a crime statistic.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. Circulation figures are from the Federal Reserve’s end-of-2025 tables. Estimates of how much currency sits abroad are research, not an official Fed census.

Most of the value of U.S. cash is already in $100s

At the end of 2025, the Federal Reserve’s currency-in-circulation tables show $1,988.8 billion of value in $100 notes, out of $2,394.9 billion in all currency. That is 83.0 percent of the value (cashscoreboard.com's arithmetic on the Fed’s figures). In piece counts, $100s were 19.9 billion of 56.6 billion notes. High-denomination notes that are no longer issued — $500 to $10,000 — were about $0.3 billion of value, or 0.0004 billion notes.

Sources: Federal Reserve, Currency in Circulation: Value; Federal Reserve, Currency in Circulation: Volume. The same table is on the Paper Cash hub.

A large share of that value is estimated to sit outside the United States

The U.S. Currency Education Program states, as a currency fact: “It is estimated that between one-half to two-thirds of the value of all U.S. currency in circulation is outside of the U.S.”

Source: U.S. Currency Education Program, Currency Facts.

A more detailed research estimate is Ruth Judson, Demand for U.S. Banknotes at Home and Abroad: A Post-Covid Update, International Finance Discussion Papers 1387 (January/March 2024). The paper is an International Finance Discussion Paper. Its analysis and conclusions are the author’s and, as the series note says, do not indicate concurrence by the Board of Governors or other members of the research staff.

Judson’s starting point, at the end of 2022, is that U.S. currency in circulation totaled about $2.2 trillion, of which $1.8 trillion, or 82 percent, was in the $100 denomination. A simple GDP-based comparison with Canada suggested that about 60 percent of all U.S. currency, and about 70 percent of $100s, were held abroad at the end of 2022, “for a total value of about $1.4 trillion.” Across the paper’s methods, estimated shares ranged from about 40 percent to 75 percent for all denominations and about 60 percent to 70 percent for $100s, centering in the 50 to 60 percent range overall and around 65 percent for $100s. In other words, about half, or a bit more than half, of U.S. currency is estimated to circulate abroad, with a somewhat larger share for $100s.

The paper also finds that demand for U.S. notes keeps growing, especially in crises, even as ordinary payments go electronic. International demand surged after 2008 and again in 2020–2021. Growth in smaller denominations has been slowing. Once a country or region begins using dollars, later crises tend to bring additional inflows.

Source: Ruth Judson, Demand for U.S. Banknotes at Home and Abroad: A Post-Covid Update, International Finance Discussion Papers 1387 (January/March 2024), including the abstract, section 3, and section 6 (“Putting It All Together”).

Overseas demand is not, on this evidence, “mostly criminal.” Judson’s framing is that dollar banknotes remain a desirable store of value and a medium of exchange when local currency or bank deposits look inferior. The Currency Education Program figure is an estimate of location, not of motive. This page does not treat foreign holdings of $100s as a proxy for crime or tax evasion.
