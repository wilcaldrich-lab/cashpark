How to spot a counterfeit

Three quick checks from the Secret Service and the Federal Reserve, what a pen can’t tell you, and what to do if you think you have a fake.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. Every claim links to its source. This page teaches the official method. It is not a product review.

The short version: Look, Feel, Tilt

The U.S. Secret Service recommends three quick checks, and the Federal Reserve says the best way to tell a genuine note is its security features, not a pen. Sources: U.S. Secret Service, Learn How to Spot Fake Money Before it Reaches Your Wallet (Nov 20, 2025); Federal Reserve, How do I determine if a banknote is genuine?.

Look

Look at the whole note. Check for foreign text, or words like “For Motion Picture Use Only” or “Replica.” Flip it over and make sure the back is properly printed, because counterfeiters sometimes spend time on the front and little on the back.

Feel

Run a finger across the note. Genuine currency feels slightly rough from the printing process and the paper. The raised, thick ink is called intaglio, and it has a texture you can feel.

Tilt

Tilt the note and watch the ink in the numerals at the lower right change color from copper to green. On a $100, check the 3-D security ribbon and the bell in the inkwell as well.

Sources: U.S. Secret Service, Learn How to Spot Fake Money Before it Reaches Your Wallet (Nov 20, 2025); U.S. Currency Education Program, Quick Glance Reference Card; U.S. Currency Education Program, Feel-Tilt-Check Card.

Check with light

Hold the note up to a light. The watermark and the security thread should be visible from both the front and the back. The thread sits in a different spot on each denomination from $5 up. Source: U.S. Currency Education Program, Feel-Tilt-Check Card.

Detection pens

The Federal Reserve says counterfeit detection pens “are not always accurate and may give you false results,” and that the best way to tell is to rely on security features such as the watermark and the security thread. Source: Federal Reserve, How do I determine if a banknote is genuine?.

What if you think you have one?

Per the Federal Reserve:

  • A counterfeit note cannot be exchanged for a genuine one, so you lose that money. It is illegal to knowingly pass counterfeit currency.
  • If you live in the United States, notify the local police right away. Try to remember what the person who passed it looked like, and if you can, write down a license plate number and vehicle description.
  • Keep the suspect note apart from your genuine currency, and release it to law enforcement as soon as possible.
  • Outside the United States, contact the nearest U.S. Secret Service field office. Contact details are on uscurrency.gov, Report a Counterfeit.

Sources: Federal Reserve, How do I determine if a banknote is genuine?; U.S. Currency Education Program, Quick Glance Reference Card.

How common are fakes, and what the Secret Service sees

  • In November 2025, a Secret Service senior counterfeit specialist said it is unlikely someone will ever encounter a counterfeit banknote.
  • The Secret Service says the most counterfeited denomination is the $20.
  • Since 2015 it has tracked “altered design notes”: bills with foreign text, bills marked as movie props, and others marked “copy” or “replica.” In 2025 they made up 10.4% of all counterfeit banknotes passed in the United States.
  • Example: late in the summer of 2025, inspectors in Florida found three boxes under a load of vegetables holding the equivalent of $5.94 million in fake $100 bills. They carried Asian writing and the words “World Universal Bank Limited,” were the wrong color, and lacked several security features. Charges were not pursued, but the notes were confiscated. The Secret Service notes that, technically, someone has to try to use fake money as genuine for it to be counterfeit.

Source: U.S. Secret Service, Learn How to Spot Fake Money Before it Reaches Your Wallet (Nov 20, 2025).
