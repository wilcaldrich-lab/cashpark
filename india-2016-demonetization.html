When a government pulls the cash: India’s 2016 demonetization

A case study, not a manifesto. What was done, what official data later showed, and what that meant for a cash-heavy informal economy.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. This is a case study from official Indian sources, not an argument for or against taking cash out of circulation.

What was done

On November 8, 2016, the Government of India announced that the two largest-denomination notes then in use, the ₹500 and the ₹1,000, were demonetized with immediate effect. They ceased to be legal tender except for a few specified purposes, such as paying utility bills. Those notes were to be deposited in banks by December 30, 2016, and restrictions were placed on cash withdrawals.

The government’s Economic Survey 2016–17 says that, “at one fell stroke, 86 percent of the cash in circulation was thereby rendered invalid.” The same chapter states the aims as fourfold: to curb corruption; counterfeiting; the use of high-denomination notes for terrorist activities; and especially the accumulation of “black money,” generated by income that had not been declared to the tax authorities.

Source: Government of India, Economic Survey 2016–17, Chapter 3, Demonetisation: To Deify or Demonize?, paragraphs 3.1–3.2.

What the official record later showed

The Reserve Bank of India’s Annual Report for 2017–18, in the Currency Management chapter, states that processing of the specified banknotes (SBNs) was completed at all of the Bank’s centres, and that “The total SBNs returned from circulation is ₹15,310.73 billion.” That official returned total is verified from the chapter (paragraph VIII.20).

The 99.3 percent figure is flagged. Contemporary coverage of the same 2017–18 annual report described ₹15,310.73 billion as 99.3 percent of the specified banknotes outstanding on November 8, 2016, and gave an outstanding total of ₹15,417.93 billion as the denominator. CashPark verified the returned total in the official chapter text. It did not find that denominator, or the 99.3 percent, in the chapter text it fetched. Until the outstanding-on-November-8 figure is confirmed in an official RBI table or the same report, this page does not treat 99.3 percent as a verified official statistic. The verified fact is the returned total: ₹15,310.73 billion.

What the official record does show is that the withdrawn notes came back into the banking system in very large volume. The Economic Survey, written in the months after the announcement, already described short-term costs and a liquidity squeeze that had been easing since the end of December 2016, and it expected a temporary decline in growth in 2016–17 before a return to trend. This page does not relitigate whether the policy “worked.” It reports what those official documents said.

Sources: Reserve Bank of India, Annual Report 2017–18, Chapter VIII, Currency Management (August 29, 2018); Government of India, Economic Survey 2016–17, Chapter 3, Demonetisation: To Deify or Demonize?.

What this page will not do

A sudden withdrawal of most of a country’s cash is a hard shock for a cash-heavy informal economy. That is the reason the episode is in this section. CashPark is not going to walk through how people tried to get around the rules, and it is not going to treat the episode as a template or a conspiracy. For how a research literature tries to size informal activity, see How big is the cash and shadow economy?. For the legal cash-acceptance picture in the United States, see CashPark, Cash laws.
