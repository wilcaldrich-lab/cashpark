Cash acceptance law tracker

Which US states, cities, and counties require in-person businesses to take cash, what each rule actually says, and where to read it. Every row links to a statute, ordinance, or official page. Where we could not confirm a date from a primary text, the row says so.

Read this first. This is a research summary, not legal advice. The laws differ a lot: Maryland covers essential goods only, between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m., for sales of $5 to under $300; Oregon lets businesses refuse $50 and $100 bills; New York, Maryland, and Illinois let stores refuse bills above $20. Many places allow a fee-free cash-to-prepaid-card machine instead of taking cash at the register. Always check the linked text. A count like "10 states" is a headline, not a uniform rule.

Related: anti-CBDC actions

These are not cash-acceptance laws. They limit a Federal Reserve-issued digital dollar, or state use of one, so they sit in their own box. We verified only the items below from official texts. A 2026 law-review survey reported 16 states with some kind of anti-CBDC measure; we have not re-counted that statute by statute, so we do not publish a state total.

ActionDateWhat it doesSource
Executive Order 14178, "Strengthening American Leadership in Digital Financial Technology"Signed Jan 23, 2025; published in the Federal Register Jan 31, 2025Directs that, except as required by law, federal agencies may not establish, issue, or promote a CBDC, and must terminate ongoing CBDC-creation plans. Revokes EO 14067.Federal Register / EO 14178
12 U.S.C. § 423, added by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, Pub. L. 119-101, title XI, § 1101Jul 11, 2026Bars the Federal Reserve Board and Reserve Banks from issuing or creating a CBDC, or anything substantially similar, directly or through an intermediary. "CBDC" is defined as a dollar-denominated U.S. currency that is a digital asset, a direct Fed liability, and widely available to the public. Exception for a dollar currency that is "open, permissionless, and private" and preserves the privacy of physical currency. Sunsets Dec 31, 2030. Because "digital asset" is defined by reference to a distributed-ledger statute, commentators have noted that a non-distributed-ledger design might fall outside the ban; that is an interpretation, not something the statute spells out in so many words.12 U.S.C. § 423
Florida SB 7054, ch. 2023-80Approved May 12, 2023; effective Jul 1, 2023Defines "central bank digital currency" and revises the UCC definition of "money" so that it does not include a CBDC. This is a commercial-code definition change, not a store-level cash-acceptance mandate.Florida Senate bill page, SB 7054 (2023)
Utah H.B. 164Effective May 1, 2024States that a central bank digital currency, as defined in the state's UCC definitions, "is not specie legal tender and is not legal tender in the state."Enrolled H.B. 164
Wyoming 2025 HB0264 (Enrolled Act No. 25)2025 general session; effective immediately on completing constitutional enactment stepsNo state agency may require payment in a CBDC for a government service, tax, or fee, or use public funds to assist in testing, adopting, or implementing a CBDC. Definition is limited to a digital medium issued directly by the Federal Reserve or an analogous federal agency.Official enrolled act
South Carolina S.163 (Act No. 208, R131)Signed May 19, 2026; effective upon approvalA "governing authority" may not accept or require payment in a CBDC, or participate in a Federal Reserve or federal-agency CBDC test. The definition carves out a digital asset backed by legal tender or government treasuries and issued by a private entity (so private stablecoins are not treated as a CBDC). The act also has separate digital-asset, self-custody, and mining provisions.Official bill / act page

The federal Payment Choice Act, which would require in-person businesses to accept cash for payments of $500 or less in the version Pew describes, has been introduced in each of the last four Congresses and has not become law (Pew Research Center, Nov 17, 2026).

How this list is kept

  • A row goes in only if we can link to a statute, ordinance, or official government page. Pew Research Center's Nov 17, 2026 analysis is used to find leads and cross-check, not as the only source.
  • Laws get delayed and amended. King County's effective date has moved twice, and D.C.'s law was paused in 2024 and resumed in 2025. Check "last checked" at the top.
