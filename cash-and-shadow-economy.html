How big is the cash and shadow economy?

“Shadow,” “informal,” and “illegal” are not the same thing. The estimates are model-based, not official GDP, and cash is used in both the measured and the unmeasured economy.

Last checked Nov 25, 2026. The figures below are research estimates, not an official GDP series. Methods disagree, and a single percentage is not a fact of nature.

Shadow, informal, and illegal are not the same thing

People use these words as if they were interchangeable. They are not. Researchers who try to measure the “shadow” or “informal” economy are usually trying to capture activity that is not fully recorded in official statistics. That can include legal work that is under-reported or done off the books, and, depending on the paper, some illegal activity. It is not a synonym for crime, and it is not a count of cash transactions.

Cash is used in both the measured economy and the unmeasured one. A restaurant that reports its sales still takes cash. A paycheck cashed at a bank is fully visible to the tax system. Treating “cash” as “underground” mixes up a payment method with a measurement problem.

Some informal activity is already inside official GDP. National accounts try to include unobserved production. The research estimates below are attempts to size what still sits outside, or partly outside, those official totals. They are model-based. They are not a line in the national accounts.

What the leading research estimates say

The most widely cited global estimates in this literature come from Leandro Medina and Friedrich Schneider. Their later paper, Medina and Schneider, Shedding Light on the Shadow Economy, CESifo Working Paper 7981 (December 2019), uses a multiple-indicators multiple-causes (MIMIC) model to estimate the shadow economy in 157 countries over 1991 to 2017. The average across all countries and years is 30.9 percent of official GDP. The average decline from 1991 to 2017 is 6.8 percentage points.

The same paper finds that the shadow economy is, on average over that period, smallest in Switzerland (6.4 percent of official GDP), the United States (7.6 percent), and Austria (7.9 percent). It is largest in Bolivia (62.9 percent), Georgia (61.7 percent), and Nigeria (56.8 percent). These are averages over 1991–2017, not a single recent year, and they are the authors’ research results, not an official IMF statistical series. Medina is an IMF economist; the paper is a CESifo working paper, not an IMF data release.

An earlier paper in the same research program, IMF Working Paper 2018/017, Medina and Schneider, Shadow Economies Around the World: What Did We Learn Over the Last 20 Years?, covers 158 countries over 1991–2015. cashscoreboard.com is citing the 2019 paper for the detailed figures above because it is the later vintage of the same method.

A second, official database with two methods

The World Bank Prospects Group, Informal Economy Database gathers up to 196 economies over 1990–2020. It includes both MIMIC and dynamic general equilibrium (DGE) estimates of informal output, plus survey-based measures of informal employment and perception surveys. The database is documented in Elgin, Kose, Ohnsorge, and Yu, Understanding Informality, CEPR Discussion Paper 16497 (2021). When you use a number from that file, the source is the database and the method (DGE or MIMIC) should travel with the figure.

cashscoreboard.com is not quoting a single United States percentage from the World Bank file on this page, because the published brief describes the methods and coverage rather than reprinting a current U.S. point estimate. The Medina/Schneider 7.6 percent figure above is an average over 1991–2017 from their 2019 paper, not a World Bank official statistic for 2020 or 2025.

How to read these numbers. Two serious research programs can disagree about the same country in the same year because they use different models and different indicators. A MIMIC estimate is not a census of unreported sales. It is a statistical construct. Do not treat 7.6 percent, or 30.9 percent, as a fact you can take to the bank. Use them as a carefully sourced range for how large the unobserved economy might be, and follow the link to the paper.

What this is not

This page does not estimate how much of the shadow economy is cash, and it does not offer advice on keeping income off the books. For what the IRS actually measures about unreported income, see the U.S. tax gap and cash-visible income. For who in the United States still lives mostly on cash, see Unbanked America.
